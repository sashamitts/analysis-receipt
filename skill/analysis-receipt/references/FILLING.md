# Filling in each line

For every line: what it is for, what a weak entry looks like, and what a strong one looks like. Strong entries use counts, names, versions and reasons. Weak entries use adjectives.

The examples are invented to show the difference.

---

## 1.1 Question and frame

**For.** Readers answer the question they are given. What sits outside it rarely shows up in the findings, so the question is the first thing someone reviewing the work needs.

- Weak: "User needs around onboarding."
- Strong: "How do new admins decide which settings to change in their first week? Given word for word to both coders and to the summarising model. Jobs-to-be-done lens. Out of scope by design: end users who are not admins."

## 1.2 What it means for the decision

**For.** It ties the analysis to its use, so the limits elsewhere on the receipt can be weighed against what is at stake.

- Weak: "Informs roadmap."
- Strong: "Supports moving bulk import into Q3. Ship with a stated limit: findings cover small teams only."

## 2.1 What was collected

**For.** It is the denominator for everything else.

- Weak: "Customer interviews."
- Strong: "24 interviews, 45 to 60 minutes, recruited from the beta waitlist by the panel vendor. 3 scheduled sessions did not take place."

## 2.2 What was read, and by which readers

**For.** Different readers see different things. Tools often read less than people assume.

- Weak: "Analysed with AI."
- Strong: "Reader 1, the researcher, read all 24 transcripts. Reader 2, the repository's summary feature (vendor model, unknown version, run 3 March), was given all 24. The tool does not show what it read. Transcripts over its length limit are cut, and 4 of the 24 were over it."

## 2.3 What was never read, and why

**For.** A gap someone admits to is better than one nobody knows about.

- Weak: (left blank)
- Strong: "2 recordings failed transcription and were not read. 1 participant withdrew consent for analysis."

## 3.1 What counts as a leftover here

**For.** It makes the leftover check repeatable.

- Strong: "Unit is a participant turn. A turn is a leftover if no finding quotes it. 812 turns, 507 leftovers."

## 3.2 What was sampled, and what it found

**For.** It is the only line that produces new evidence. It turns "we probably got most of it" into a number with a stated uncertainty.

- Weak: "Spot-checked the transcripts."
- Strong: "Random sample of 60 of 507 leftovers, seed 4417, read by the researcher. 9 were examples of existing findings, 0 held a new idea, 51 held nothing relevant. Upper bound on the share of leftovers holding a new idea: under 5% at 95%."

If the sample does find new ideas, write them up, update the findings, and say so here. That is the check working, not failing.

## 3.3 Targeted searches

**For.** Random samples bound the common misses and miss the rare ones.

- Strong: "One model briefed to find participants the question does not fit. It found 2; both checked against the transcripts; 1 held and is reported as finding 7."

## 3.4 What was not checked

**For.** The reader of the report should not have to infer it.

- Weak: "Some limitations apply."
- Strong: "Not checked: the 180 waitlist users not interviewed; a second coder; the 3 non-English interviews."

## 4.1 What each claim rests on

**For.** It shows how much each finding carries, and whether anyone looked for cases against it.

- Weak: "Most users want X."
- Strong: "Claim 3, admins avoid bulk import after one bad experience: 9 of 24 participants, counted from coded turns. Cases against: 2 who used it after a failure; both had a colleague who restored data for them. Reported as a condition."

## 4.2 Claims that did not hold

**For.** Models and people both propose claims that do not survive a close reading. Recording them shows the checking happened.

- Strong: "The summary claimed admins distrust automation. Checked against all 24 transcripts. 3 said so; 11 described relying on it. Dropped."
